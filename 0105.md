# Hypotheses and Experimental Designs: Study Efficiency Optimization

---

## Research Story 1 (High Priority) — Note-Formatting Methods

### Hypothesis

**H1 (Primary):** Structured note-formatting methods (Cornell method, outline method, mind-mapping) will lead to significantly higher review test scores compared to unstructured (free-form / verbatim transcription) note-taking.

**H1a:** Among structured methods, the Cornell method will produce the highest review efficiency (test score per minute of review time) because its built-in cue-and-recall layout facilitates active retrieval.

**H1b:** Mind-mapping will outperform other methods on conceptual-integration questions (e.g., essay prompts requiring cross-topic synthesis) due to its spatial organization of relationships between ideas.

**H0 (Null):** There is no significant difference in review test scores across different note-formatting methods.

### Experimental Design

**Design Type:** Between-subjects, single-factor with four levels.

**Independent Variable (IV):** Note-formatting method, with four conditions:
- Condition A: Cornell method
- Condition B: Outline method
- Condition C: Mind-mapping
- Condition D: Free-form (control group)

**Dependent Variables (DVs):**
- *Primary:* Review test score (0–100), a composite of factual recall, conceptual understanding, and application questions, administered 48 hours after the initial learning session.
- *Secondary:* Review efficiency (test score ÷ self-reported review time in minutes).
- *Exploratory:* Self-reported cognitive load (NASA-TLX adapted), perceived ease of review (7-point Likert scale).

**Participants:** 120 undergraduate students (30 per condition), screened for prior familiarity with note-taking methods. Random assignment to conditions with stratification by baseline GPA.

**Procedure:**
1. **Pre-screening:** Assess baseline academic performance and prior exposure to note-taking methods. Exclude participants with extensive Cornell/mind-mapping experience.
2. **Training session (Day 1, 30 min):** All participants receive a 15-minute standardized training on their assigned note-taking method, followed by a 5-minute practice exercise to ensure procedural competence.
3. **Learning session (Day 1, 45 min):** All participants watch the same 30-minute video lecture on an unfamiliar topic (e.g., introductory neuroscience). They take notes using their assigned method. A 5-minute buffer is provided at the end for note completion.
4. **Distraction phase (Day 1, 10 min):** Participants complete a non-verbal puzzle task to clear working memory and prevent rote rehearsal.
5. **Review session (Day 3, 20 min):** Participants are given their own notes back and instructed to review for an upcoming test. Review time is self-paced and recorded.
6. **Test (Day 3, 30 min):** All participants complete the same standardized test covering factual recall (multiple-choice), conceptual understanding (short-answer), and application (essay) items.
7. **Post-test survey:** Cognitive load, perceived ease of review, and method satisfaction ratings are collected.

**Controls:**
- Same video lecture for all conditions.
- Same review duration cap (20 min).
- Same test for all conditions.
- Participants cannot access external materials during review or test.

**Analysis Plan:**
- One-way ANOVA on review test scores across four conditions.
- Planned contrasts: structured vs. unstructured (H1), Cornell vs. other structured (H1a), mind-mapping vs. others on essay items (H1b).
- ANCOVA with baseline GPA as covariate for sensitivity check.
- Effect size: η² (eta-squared) for overall effect, Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons.

## Research Story 2 (Medium Priority) — Study-Planning Tools and Learning Productivity

### Hypothesis

**H1 (Primary):** Learners using digital study-planning tools (e.g., calendar-based scheduling apps, task-management platforms) will demonstrate higher learning productivity — measured as task completion rate and time-on-task ratio — compared to learners using paper-based planners or no formal planning tool.

**H1a:** Digital tools with reminder/notification features will reduce procrastination (shorter delay between planned and actual study start time) compared to paper-based tools, which lack automated prompts.

**H1b:** The "planning overhead" (time spent organizing vs. time spent learning) will be lowest for the no-tool control group, but their net productive learning time will be lowest due to poor task prioritization and frequent context-switching.

**H0 (Null):** There is no significant difference in learning productivity across different study-planning tool conditions.

### Experimental Design

**Design Type:** Between-subjects, single-factor with three levels, conducted over a 2-week study period.

**Independent Variable (IV):** Study-planning tool, with three conditions:
- Condition A: Digital planning tool (e.g., Google Calendar + Todoist, with notifications enabled)
- Condition B: Paper-based planner (paper diary / bullet journal)
- Condition C: No formal planning tool (control — ad hoc, self-directed)

**Dependent Variables (DVs):**
- *Primary:* Learning productivity index, a composite score derived from: (tasks completed ÷ tasks planned) × (time spent learning ÷ total session time).
- *Secondary:* Procrastination delay (minutes between scheduled start and actual start, logged daily), self-reported study satisfaction (7-point Likert scale).
- *Exploratory:* Planning overhead ratio (time spent planning ÷ total study session time), number of context switches per study session (self-reported).

**Participants:** 90 undergraduate students (30 per condition), currently enrolled in at least one course with self-directed study requirements. Random assignment, blocked by self-reported baseline self-regulation score (Short Self-Regulation Questionnaire, SSRQ).

**Procedure:**
1. **Pre-screening (Day 0):** Administer SSRQ and baseline study-habits questionnaire. Participants list all upcoming study tasks for the next two weeks.
2. **Tool training (Day 0, 20 min):** Groups A and B receive standardized training on their assigned tool. Group C receives no training.
3. **2-week study period (Day 1–14):**
   - Each evening, participants plan the next day's study tasks using their assigned tool (Groups A and B) or their own method (Group C).
   - Participants log daily: planned tasks, completed tasks, planned start time, actual start time, total study duration, and number of interruptions/context switches.
   - Group A's digital tool logs are verified against app timestamps; Groups B and C use a structured daily diary (paper or digital form).
4. **Midpoint check-in (Day 7):** Brief survey on adherence and tool usability. Address any technical issues.
5. **Exit survey (Day 14):** Final productivity self-assessment, tool satisfaction, and open-ended feedback on the planning experience.

**Controls:**
- All participants complete the same daily logging form (regardless of planning condition) to ensure consistent DV measurement.
- Study period length is identical (14 days).
- Participants are instructed not to change their planning tool during the study.

**Analysis Plan:**
- One-way ANOVA on the learning productivity index across three conditions.
- Planned contrast for digital vs. paper (H1a) on procrastination delay.
- Repeated-measures ANOVA on daily productivity trends (Day 1–14) to examine tool adoption/learning effects.
- Mediation analysis: does planning overhead mediate the relationship between tool type and productivity?

## Research Story 3 (High Priority) — Spaced Repetition vs. Daily Review

### Hypothesis

**H1 (Primary):** Spaced repetition scheduling (expanding intervals: 1 day → 3 days → 7 days → 14 days) will produce significantly higher long-term retention scores at a 30-day follow-up compared to daily massed review.

**H1a:** The spaced-repetition group will show a slower forgetting curve (smaller slope of retention decay) between the immediate post-test and the 30-day follow-up compared to the daily-review group.

**H1b:** The daily-review group will outperform the spaced-repetition group on the immediate post-test (Day 7) due to higher rehearsal frequency, but this advantage will reverse by the 30-day follow-up — a classic spacing-effect crossover interaction.

**H0 (Null):** There is no significant difference in long-term retention between spaced repetition and daily review schedules.

### Experimental Design

**Design Type:** Mixed design — 2 (review method: between-subjects) × 4 (testing occasion: within-subjects).

**Independent Variables (IVs):**
- *IV1 (Between-subjects):* Review method
  - Condition A: Spaced repetition (expanding intervals — review on Days 2, 5, 12, 26 after initial learning on Day 1)
  - Condition B: Daily massed review (review every day from Day 2 through Day 7, then no further review)
- *IV2 (Within-subjects):* Testing occasion — immediate post-test (Day 7), 7-day follow-up (Day 14), 30-day follow-up (Day 37), and 90-day follow-up (Day 97).

**Dependent Variables (DVs):**
- *Primary:* Retention score on a standardized 50-item test (factual recall + conceptual application), scored 0–100.
- *Secondary:* Forgetting rate (slope of retention curve from Day 7 to Day 97), relearning speed at Day 97 (additional study time needed to regain Day 7 performance).
- *Exploratory:* Metacognitive accuracy (predicted vs. actual test score at each time point), subjective judgment of learning (JO L) ratings.

**Participants:** 80 undergraduate students (40 per condition), no prior knowledge of the learning material. Random assignment, matched on baseline memory span (digit span test).

**Procedure:**
1. **Baseline assessment (Day 0):** Digit span (forward and backward), vocabulary pre-test to screen for prior knowledge of target material. Participants scoring above 20% on the pre-test are excluded.
2. **Initial learning session (Day 1, 60 min):** All participants study 40 Swahili–English word pairs (standardized Ebbinghaus-style paired-associate material). Participants study until they reach a criterion of 90% correct recall in a single pass (dropout if not reached within 60 min).
3. **Review phase (Days 2–26):**
   - *Condition A (Spaced):* Four review sessions on Days 2, 5, 12, and 26. Each session presents all 40 pairs via computerized flashcard with self-paced study until 90% correct.
   - *Condition B (Daily):* Six daily review sessions on Days 2–7. Same flashcard procedure. No review after Day 7.
   - Total review sessions: 4 (spaced) vs. 6 (daily). Total review time is tracked as a covariate.
4. **Testing occasions:** All participants complete the same 50-item cued-recall test (Swahili word presented → recall English translation) on Days 7, 14, 37, and 97. At each test, participants also provide a JO L (0–100 prediction of their score).
5. **Relearning phase (Day 97):** After the final test, participants restudy all pairs to criterion. Time-to-criterion is recorded as a measure of savings.

**Controls:**
- Identical learning materials (same 40 Swahili–English pairs).
- Same initial learning criterion (90% correct).
- Same review procedure (computerized flashcard to 90% criterion).
- Same tests at all four time points.
- Participants are unaware of testing schedule to prevent differential rehearsal.

**Analysis Plan:**
- 2 × 4 mixed ANOVA on retention scores, with review method as between-subjects factor and testing occasion as within-subjects factor.
- Follow-up: simple main effects of review method at each testing occasion (H1b predicts daily > spaced at Day 7, spaced > daily at Day 37 and Day 97).
- Linear mixed-effects model of forgetting slopes (H1a), with random intercepts and slopes per participant.
- ANCOVA controlling for total review time to rule out the confound of differential total exposure.
- Correlation between JO L and actual scores to assess metacognitive calibration in each condition.

## Research Story 4 (Low Priority) — Background Noise and Focus

### Hypothesis

**H1 (Primary):** Moderate ambient background noise (white noise or nature sounds at ~45–55 dB) will lead to better sustained-focus performance (higher accuracy and lower reaction-time variability on a continuous performance task) compared to complete silence (< 30 dB) and disruptive noise (conversational chatter at ~65–70 dB).

**H1a:** The relationship between noise level and focus follows an inverted-U (Yerkes–Dodson) curve: both under-arousal (silence) and over-arousal (disruptive noise) impair performance, while moderate arousal (ambient noise) optimizes it.

**H1b:** Participants in the disruptive-noise condition will show greater subjective fatigue and frustration (NASA-TLX subscales) compared to the ambient-noise and silence conditions, independent of their objective performance.

**H0 (Null):** There is no significant difference in sustained-focus performance across different background noise conditions.

### Experimental Design

**Design Type:** Within-subjects (repeated measures), counterbalanced, with three noise conditions.

**Independent Variable (IV):** Background noise type, with three levels:
- Condition A: White noise (continuous, 50 dB SPL)
- Condition B: Complete silence (< 30 dB SPL; noise-cancelling headphones, no audio playback)
- Condition C: Disruptive noise (pre-recorded cafeteria chatter, 65–70 dB SPL)

**Dependent Variables (DVs):**
- *Primary:* Sustained-focus performance measured via a 20-minute Continuous Performance Task (CPT) — mean hit rate (correct detections), mean reaction time (RT), and RT variability (standard deviation of RT across blocks).
- *Secondary:* Self-reported focus (7-point Likert scale after each condition), NASA-TLX workload subscales (mental demand, frustration, perceived effort).
- *Exploratory:* Heart-rate variability (HRV) via wearable (if available), error-type analysis (omission vs. commission errors), self-reported preference ranking of the three noise conditions at study conclusion.

**Participants:** 45 university students (within-subjects design reduces required N relative to between-subjects). Screened for normal hearing, no diagnosed ADHD or attention disorders (to reduce confound variance). Participants must not regularly use background noise during study (to avoid familiarity confound).

**Procedure:**
1. **Screening (Day 0):** Hearing test (pure-tone audiometry via calibrated app), attention-disorder screening (ASRS-v1.1), baseline study-environment questionnaire.
2. **Experimental sessions (three sessions, 1–3 days apart):**
   - Each participant completes all three noise conditions, one per session, in counterbalanced order (Latin square assignment).
   - Sessions are scheduled at the same time of day (±1 hour) to control for circadian effects on attention.
   - **Per session (total 35 min):**
     - Setup and noise acclimation (5 min): participant puts on headphones, the assigned noise condition begins, and they sit quietly.
     - CPT task (20 min): standardized computer-based continuous performance task. Stimuli (target letters) appear at 1–2 s random intervals; participant presses spacebar for targets only. 600 trials total, 20% targets.
     - Post-task survey (5 min): NASA-TLX, focus rating, and perceived distraction level.
     - Washout (5 min): silence and light reading to reset before leaving.
3. **Exit interview (after final session):** Rank the three noise conditions from most to least preferred for studying. Open-ended feedback on subjective experience.

**Controls:**
- Same CPT task and parameters across all sessions.
- Same hardware (headphones, computer, testing room).
- Same time of day per participant.
- Counterbalanced condition order.
- Sound levels calibrated with a sound-level meter before each session.
- Experimenter blind to condition order during data processing.

**Analysis Plan:**
- One-way repeated-measures ANOVA on each DV (hit rate, mean RT, RT variability) across three noise conditions.
- Polynomial contrast (quadratic) to test the inverted-U hypothesis (H1a): expected pattern is silence < ambient > disruptive.
- Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction: ambient vs. silence, ambient vs. disruptive, silence vs. disruptive.
- Repeated-measures ANOVA on NASA-TLX frustration and fatigue subscales across conditions.
- Friedman test (non-parametric) on preference rankings from exit interview.
- Effect size: generalized η² for repeated-measures ANOVA.
